**Why LoRA Fine-Tuning Is the Only Viable Approach for Low-Resource Cebuano Language Modeling**

Training a large language model to generate coherent text in a specific language requires enormous amounts of data and compute. Kaplan et al. (2020) demonstrated that model performance scales predictably as a power law across three dimensions: the number of model parameters, the size of the training dataset, and the amount of compute used. This means there are no shortcuts — doubling the data or the model size yields a predictable but limited gain, and no combination of clever tricks replaces raw scale. Hoffmann et al. (2022) refined this into a concrete, actionable ratio known as the Chinchilla scaling law: to train a model optimally from scratch, you need approximately 20 tokens of training data per model parameter. DistilGPT2 has 82 million parameters. By this ratio, training it from scratch to competitive performance would require roughly 1.64 billion tokens of Cebuano text. Our combined corpus — drawn from CebuaNER and the cebuano-readability dataset — contains nowhere near this amount, likely only a few hundred thousand to a few million tokens. Full pretraining from scratch is therefore not simply impractical due to hardware limitations; it is fundamentally unviable because the data does not exist in sufficient quantity for the Cebuano language.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) sidesteps this problem entirely by exploiting what DistilGPT2 already knows. The model’s 82 million pretrained weights encode general language structure learned from billions of English tokens — patterns of syntax, subword co-occurrence, and semantic association. These representations are not English-specific in the deepest sense; they encode how language works, and that knowledge transfers. Rather than discarding and relearning all of this from scratch, LoRA freezes all 82 million pretrained weights and injects small trainable adapter matrices into the model’s attention layers. With a rank setting of r=8, each adapter pair contains approximately 12,288 parameters instead of the 589,824 that a full attention matrix holds. Across all layers, this means only roughly 300,000–800,000 parameters are trained — about 1% of the full model. The model is not learning what a sentence is. It is learning to steer its existing, deeply encoded language representations toward Cebuano patterns.

This difference in approach produces a qualitatively different compute regime. Full pretraining at the scale Chinchilla demands requires thousands of GPU-hours and terabytes of data — resources unavailable to most research teams and entirely unavailable for a low-resource language like Cebuano. LoRA fine-tuning on our corpus runs in a few CPU-hours on a standard laptop because: (a) only approximately 1% of parameters are trainable, meaning the model holds 99% of its weights frozen and computes no gradients for them; (b) the training corpus is orders of magnitude smaller than what full pretraining requires; and (c) gradient computation flows only through the small adapter matrices, not through the full 82-million-parameter network. It is important to acknowledge the honest tradeoff: the resulting model is not a production-grade Cebuano language model. It will not match the fluency of a model trained on billions of Cebuano tokens, because such a corpus does not exist. What it does demonstrate, concretely and reproducibly, is the adaptation phase of the LLM development lifecycle — the process by which a pretrained model is steered toward a new domain — and that is the core learning objective of this project.
